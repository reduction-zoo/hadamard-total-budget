                     NP-hardness of total-rank-budget
                        Hadamard decomposition

                     Working manuscript · Awaiting independent human expert review

Abstract
We study the minimum sum of the ranks of two real matrices whose entrywise product is a prescribed
rational matrix. We give a deterministic polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to deciding whether this
sum is at most an input budget. The result holds for nonnegative integer input matrices and unrestricted-
sign real factors. A square identity block forces every feasible allocation of the budget to have equal
ranks. Additional pair-sum rows impose a partially specified Cartesian frame while retaining two-
choice selectors. Calibration and a cycle of weighted rows encode subset product. The construction has
a general converse for every admissible rank allocation. A deterministic decoder recovers a satisfying
assignment from every valid decomposition encoded over one real algebraic primitive element. We give
explicit polynomial bit bounds and separate the proof from finite computational checks.

1. Introduction
Hadamard decomposition represents a matrix as the entrywise product of low-rank matrices. Ciaperoni,
Gionis and Mannila studied this representation for data analysis and left the exact real feasibility
problem’s complexity unresolved [1]. Subsequent numerical work explicitly compares decompositions
under a total rank budget [2]. For a matrix with 𝜇 rows and 𝑁 columns, factors of widths 𝑝, 𝑞 use
(𝜇 + 𝑁 )(𝑝 + 𝑞) scalar parameters in their dense parameterization. This makes the rank sum a natural
representation cost.
For a rational matrix 𝑀 ∈ ℚ 𝜇×𝑁 , define
                               𝒞︀0 (𝑀 ) =    min (rank(𝐴) + rank(𝐵)),                                 (1)
                                            𝑀=𝐴⊙𝐵

where 𝐴, 𝐵 ∈ ℝ𝜇×𝑁 and ⊙ denotes entrywise multiplication. There are exactly two factors. Their
entries may have arbitrary signs, and rank zero is allowed; in particular, 𝒞︀0 (0) = 0. The decision input
is (𝑀 , 𝑏), where 𝑏 is a nonnegative integer in binary. We ask whether 𝒞︀0 (𝑀 ) ≤ 𝑏.
A hardness result with prescribed bounds on the two ranks would not by itself settle this question.
A matrix infeasible at one allocation could be feasible at another allocation with the same sum. Our
construction excludes all such alternatives through equality in the rank-product bound.
Theorem 1 (Main result). Deciding 𝒞︀0 (𝑀 ) ≤ 𝑏 is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time
many-one reductions, even for nonnegative integer matrices 𝑀 . A 3-SAT formula with 𝑛 occurring
variables and 𝑚 clauses maps to a matrix with a leading identity of order 𝑠2 and budget
                             𝐾 = max(3, 2𝑛 + 2𝑚),        𝑠 = 2𝐾, 𝑏 = 2𝑠.                              (2)

It is feasible exactly when the formula is satisfiable. Every feasible decomposition of the constructed
matrix has both factor ranks equal to 𝑠. From every valid algebraically encoded decomposition, a
deterministic algorithm recovers a satisfying assignment in time polynomial in the source length plus
the supplied witness length.
The reduction uses the standard Karp framework [3]. Prime exponents encode 3-SAT as subset product.
The matrix construction supplies the discrete choices: an incomplete Cartesian frame forces anchor
directions while leaving two orientations at each selector. Weighted rows make the product of the
selected integers equal a target. The frame, together with budget saturation, is the mechanism that
addresses arbitrary rank allocations.

4. Structure of every feasible decomposition
4.1. Product bases and pair sums
By Lemma 1, any feasible witness has widths (𝑠, 𝑠). A nonzero tensor of the form 𝑢 ⊗ 𝑤 is a product
vector; its coordinate matrix has rank one. Associate a row tensor 𝑢𝐷 ⊗ 𝑤𝐷 to every row 𝐷, and a
column tensor 𝑣𝐽 ⊗ 𝜁𝐽 to every column 𝐽 . Their pairing equals 𝑀𝐷,𝐽 . The tensor interpretation is
consistent with the rank-one reshape formulation in [4].
The leading identity pairs 𝑁 row tensors with 𝑁 column tensors as 𝐼𝑁 . Since dim(ℝ𝑠 ⊗ ℝ𝑠 ) = 𝑁 ,
these families are dual bases. Let 𝑧𝐽 denote the identity row tensor at index 𝐽 . Every later row with
coefficients 𝑚𝐽 therefore has the uniquely determined tensor ∑𝐽 𝑚𝐽 𝑧𝐽 .
Two independent product vectors have a product-vector sum exactly when they share a left or a right
factor line. If neither line is shared, linear maps on the two independent pairs send their sum to a 2 ×
2 identity, contradicting rank one. Sharing a line gives the converse by distributivity.
A clique of at least three independent product vectors with product-vector pair sums has one common
factor line. Fix a pair sharing a left line and having distinct right lines. A third vector without that left
line would have to share both distinct right lines. This is impossible, so the third vector shares the left
line. The argument extends to the whole clique, and exchanging left and right gives the other case. Its
common side is unique, since sharing both lines would make the vectors proportional.

4.2. Forcing the anchor frame
Lemma 3 (Anchor frame). After possibly exchanging the two factor spaces, there are bases
𝛼0 , …, 𝛼𝑠−1 and 𝛽0 , …, 𝛽𝑠−1 such that every anchor at index (𝑎, 𝑐) is a nonzero scalar multiple of 𝛼𝑎 ⊗
𝛽𝑐 . Each pair 𝑋𝑖 , 𝑌𝑖 occupies the two opposite corners between the left lines 𝛼0 , 𝛼1 and right lines
𝛽2𝑖 , 𝛽2𝑖+1 .
Proof. Every complete anchor row has 𝑠 members, and every anchor column has 𝑠 − 1 members. Their
pair constraints and the preceding clique argument give each a common side. A complete row and a
column intersect once. If their sides agreed, their common lines would coincide at that intersection.
Their union would place 2𝑠 − 2 > 𝑠 independent basis vectors on one factor line, in a subspace of
dimension 𝑠. Thus their sides are opposite.
Every complete row intersects every column. All complete rows consequently have the same orien­
tation, and all columns the opposite orientation. A top anchor row has 𝐾 members and intersects
each applicable column once. Equal orientation would confine 𝐾 + 𝑠 − 2 > 𝑠 independent vectors to
dimension 𝑠, because 𝐾 ≥ 3. Both top rows therefore orient like the complete rows. After a possible
global swap, write the anchor factors on the lines 𝛼𝑎 and 𝛽𝑐 as in the statement.
The 𝑠 anchors in a complete row are independent, forcing the 𝑠 right lines to be independent. In
particular, each 𝐿𝑖 , 𝑅𝑖 pair has distinct right lines. Suppose the two top left lines coincided. A common
neighbor 𝑋𝑖 or 𝑌𝑖 without that left line would have to share both distinct right lines of its anchors.
Hence every local neighbor would have the common top left line. All 𝑁 basis vectors would then have
left factors in the span of at most 𝑠 − 1 lines, contradicting their full span.

6. Recovery and bit complexity
6.1. Decoding an arbitrary valid witness
The two identity rows 𝐿0 , 𝐿1 , with row-major indices 0, 2, share a factor line on the oriented left side
and have independent factors on the other side. Test whether rows 0 and 2 of 𝑈 are proportional. If
so, use 𝑈 for decoding; otherwise use 𝑊 . This determines the correct side even after a global factor
exchange. Call the chosen matrix 𝑄.
For each assignment item 𝑖, test whether row 𝑋𝑖 , of index 2𝑖 + 1, is proportional to row 𝐿0 in 𝑄. By
Equation 14,
                              𝜀𝑖 = 1 ⇔ 𝑄𝑋𝑖 ,∗ is proportional to 𝑄𝐿0 ,∗ .                              (19)

Read the true item in each variable pair. Lemma 2 guarantees one selected assignment item per pair and
satisfaction of every clause. The predicate depends on factor lines, so arbitrary invertible coordinate
changes and nonzero gauges do not affect it.

6.2. Exact algebraic zero tests
The finite witness encoding uses one real algebraic primitive element 𝜃. It supplies a nonzero dense
integer polynomial 𝑃 and rational non-root endpoints 𝑎 < 𝑏 isolating exactly one distinct real root 𝜃.
Every factor coordinate is a dense rational polynomial evaluated at that root. The defining polynomial
may be reducible or have repeated roots. Rational numerators and denominators are written as integer
literals, without compressed exponents.
For a nonzero vector 𝑢, choose its first nonzero coordinate 𝑢𝑗 . Another vector 𝑣 is proportional precisely
when
                               𝑢𝑗 𝑣𝑙 − 𝑣𝑗 𝑢𝑙 = 0   for every coordinate 𝑙.                             (20)

Every identity-factor row is nonzero. To test a polynomial 𝐷 at 𝜃, use
                             𝐷(𝜃) = 0 ⇔ gcd(𝑃 , 𝐷) has a root in (𝑎, 𝑏).                               (21)

All roots of the gcd are roots of 𝑃 , so the isolating premise makes its root count zero or one. This avoids
factorization of 𝑃 and construction of a new number field.
The executable takes the last nonzero subresultant for the gcd, clears rational denominators, removes
multiplicities with the derivative gcd, and counts roots with a sign-corrected subresultant Sturm
sequence. Endpoint variation counts omit zero evaluations. The required coefficient and algebraic-
comparison bounds are standard [5]. The implementation calls SymPy’s Poly.subresultants and
sturm_amv(method=1) the latter uses controlled remainders and the Sturm signs discussed in Section
2.4.3 of [6]. No feasibility solver runs inside construction or recovery.

The converse holds over all real factors. Executable recovery concerns the specified finite algebraic
encoding. A nonempty real solution set of the rational factorization equations has a real-algebraic point,
by real closed field transfer; this does not assert a polynomial certificate bound for arbitrary target
inputs. Malformed source strings map outside the target language. Canonical renaming of occurring
variables is polynomial-time. These observations complete Theorem 1.

7. Related work and scope
The total rank budget and extremal identity constructions are prior results: Theorem 1 and Appendices
A–B of [2] study rank-product expressivity, including optimization over the number of factors. Our
problem keeps exactly two factors and tests a prescribed matrix. Extremal identity realizations do not
impose the extra anchor and cycle constraints needed in the reduction.
The rank-one reshape viewpoint is also prior work [4]. Hardness of matrix signing and entrywise
powers [8] ties factor magnitudes or coordinates; dropping those restrictions does not supply a converse
for independent Hadamard factors. Generic Hadamard–Hitchcock identifiability [9] addresses different
hypotheses and does not decide exact membership for these specially constrained matrices.
The new construction combines the incomplete anchor frame with two-factor budget saturation. Its
selector-cycle mechanism extends the preceding fixed-width construction in this research project; it
is not claimed as a second independent discovery. This paper proves the strengthened theorem self-
containedly. Primary-literature searches through 15 September 2026 found no matching total-budget
hardness theorem within the reviewed coverage. Unindexed manuscripts, exhaustive citation closure
and overlooked implications of broader representation results remain outside that coverage.
The result concerns exact minimum rank sum. It establishes neither approximation hardness nor strong
NP-hardness, hardness for a variable number of factors, or NP membership for unrestricted target
inputs. Both ranks grow with the source. The nominal parameter count motivating Equation 1 does not
measure minimal bit storage, statistical generalization, or typical training behavior. Independent human
assessment of correctness and novelty remains necessary.

A.1. Evidence and limitations
The implementation uses Python 3.14.7 and SymPy 1.14.0. Prepared tests use Z3 5.1.0; this document uses
Typst 0.15.1. The generic target oracle enumerates all unordered legal rank allocations and solves their
defining quartic equations. Swapping factors makes this enumeration complete. An unknown outcome is
an error, not a NO answer. Its hand-verifiable checks cover zero, rank-one and identity cases, algebraic
witnesses, invalid products and budget violations.
The prepared suite injects six formulas and makes eight recovery calls, using both factor orders. A separately
implemented verifier checks fourteen distinct formulas, twelve YES and two NO, and makes seventy recovery
calls, including four algebraic gauges. Cases include all eight sign patterns of a one-variable clause, contra­
dictory repeated unit clauses, and mixed cases with up to three variables and 𝐾 ≤ 8. The largest target has
3720 rows and 256 columns.
For these larger outputs, each checker validates the entire matrix against the frame family before solving its
extracted subset-product coefficients. The prepared checker uses Boolean-controlled linear prefix equations
in Z3. The other uses meet-in-the-middle enumeration and exhaustive source assignments. Both construct
target factors independently of the candidate and check all matrix entries exactly before invoking recovery
in a fresh process. Their structural NO conclusions depend on Lemmas 1–4; agreement of finite oracles does
not prove those lemmas.
Witness variants include alternative subsets, coordinate changes mixing active and frame directions, signed
row and column gauges, and entire-factor exchange. Algebraic tests use positive and negative quadratic
roots, a cubic root and a repeated defining polynomial. A separate reviewer checked the general proof and
built further full witnesses without importing candidate implementations. Those checks used negative prefix
parameters, dense coordinate changes, an isolated fifth root with a reducible repeated defining polynomial,
and degree-14 coordinate representatives, in both factor orders. The review requested no repair. Agent review
and finite tests do not replace human expert verification.
